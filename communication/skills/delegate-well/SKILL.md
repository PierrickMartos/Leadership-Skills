# Delegate Well

Delegation fails at the handoff far more often than at the doing. The person did what they
thought you asked. You meant something else. Now you are redoing it, and they have learned
that your instructions cannot be trusted on their own.

Your job is to close that gap before the work starts. Two moves: check the handoff is even the
right call, then load the person with enough context that they can make good decisions when
you are not in the room.

The core test, borrowed from Wes Kao: if you cannot answer these six questions yourself, the
person you are delegating to definitely cannot.

1. What does this person already know, and what is new for them?
2. Why are we doing this?
3. What do they need in order to start?
4. What does great look like?
5. What is the timeline and priority level?
6. What is most likely to go wrong?

Source: https://newsletter.weskao.com/p/before-you-delegate-ask-yourself

---

## Step 1: Triage before you brief

Two quick gates. Most of the time both pass in seconds. When one fails, catching it here saves
a wasted week.

**Gate 1: is this delegable at all?**

Keep it if it is a decision only you have the authority or context to make, if it is feedback
or a performance conversation about a person, or if the work is so entangled with your own
in-flight thinking that briefing it would take longer than doing it and the task is genuinely
one-off.

That last one is the trap. "Faster to do it myself" is true exactly once. If the task will
recur, the briefing is an investment, not a cost. Say that out loud to the user when you see it.

**Gate 2: is this the right person?**

Sort the work into one of three buckets and say which one you are in:

- **In their wheelhouse.** They have done this or its close cousin. Brief light, autonomy high.
- **Stretch.** New shape of work, but adjacent to what they know. This is where growth happens
  and also where rework happens. Brief heavy, check in early, and tell them explicitly that
  this is a stretch so they know to ask questions rather than bluff.
- **Stretch too far.** Missing a prerequisite skill, missing the relationships to get it done,
  or already underwater. Delegating here is setting someone up to fail. Either pair them with
  someone, cut the scope to the part they can own, or pick someone else.

If the user is delegating primarily to develop someone, name that. The brief changes: you give
more of the why, less of the how, and you build in a checkpoint you would not otherwise need.

## Step 2: Run the six questions and find the gaps

Work through the six with the user. Ask only what you cannot infer from what they have already
told you. Do not interrogate someone who has already given you the picture.

For each question, you are looking for a specific answer, not a plausible one. Vague answers
here become rework later, so push once when you get one.

### 1. What does this person already know? What is new?

Two failure modes, opposite directions. Over-explaining what they already know reads as
condescension and gets skimmed, which means they skim the part they needed. Under-explaining
context you have absorbed over months and forgotten you ever learned leaves them guessing.

Name what is new for them specifically. "You have run these before, the difference this time is
the data lives in Looker, not the spreadsheet."

### 2. Why are we doing this?

This is the highest-leverage question and the most commonly skipped. The why is what lets them
make a good call on the fifteen small decisions you did not anticipate. Without it they either
follow your instructions off a cliff, or stop and ask, and now you are in the loop anyway.

Push past the proximate reason to the real one. Not "the exec team asked for it" but "the exec
team is deciding in two weeks whether to keep funding this, and this is the evidence."

A weak why: "we need to update the onboarding docs."
A strong why: "three of the last four new hires got stuck on the same setup step and lost a day
each. We want the next hire to get to their first commit without asking anyone."

### 3. What do they need in order to start?

Mise en place. Gather it before the handoff, not after they ask. Every missing item is a day
lost and a small tax on their momentum.

Run the list: tool and system access, the relevant docs and previous examples, the data or
files, budget and spending authority, the names of people they will need and a warm intro to
each, and the decisions already made that they should not reopen.

That last item is easy to forget and expensive to skip. Tell them what is settled so they do
not spend a day relitigating it.

**If the work recurs, you are transferring ownership, not lending out a task.** Access alone
leaves them doing your job without your standing, which is the version of delegation that
quietly fails. Hand over the position too:

- **Standing with the stakeholders.** Tell the people who consume this that this person now
  owns it, in front of them, once. Otherwise everyone keeps routing around them back to you.
- **The calendar.** The recurring meeting, the standing invite, the slot on someone else's
  agenda. If it lives on your calendar, so does the work.
- **The decision rights.** Which calls they now make without you. Vague ownership plus retained
  veto is the worst of both.
- **The runbook, written by them.** If one exists, hand it over. If one does not, have them
  write it as they go rather than writing it for them. It costs you nothing, it is more
  accurate than yours would be, and drafting it forces them to surface the gaps in what you
  told them while there is still time to fix it.

### 4. What does great look like?

Show, do not tell. A past example, a mock-up, a screenshot, a competitor's version, a rough
sketch. Pattern matching beats description.

An imperfect example is still far better than an adjective. "It is like this deck, except the
data section goes first and it should be half the length" transmits more in one line than a
paragraph of criteria.

If no example exists, describe the artifact concretely: the format, the length, the audience,
and where it will end up. "A one-page memo the leadership team reads cold in the Monday
meeting" is a spec. "A good analysis" is not.

Say what done means too. Is a draft for your review done, or is shipped done?

### 5. What is the timeline and priority?

Give a real date, not a temperature. "ASAP" and "urgent" are not timelines, and if everything
is urgent, they will guess at the order and guess wrong.

Two parts:
- **When.** A date, or a window: "by Thursday", "some time in the next two weeks".
- **Against what.** Where this sits relative to the other things on their plate. If it should
  displace something, say what. If it should not, say that too.

Then name the quality bar for that timeline. Fast and rough, and thorough and polished, are
different tasks. If you want it Thursday, say what you are willing to trade to get it Thursday.

### 6. What is most likely to go wrong?

You have the pattern recognition here and they do not. Spend it. This is where you say the
quiet parts: the stakeholder who will want to reopen a settled decision, the data source that
looks right and is not, the approach that seems obvious and has already been tried and failed.

Stating what you do not want is fair game and often the fastest way to prevent the common
failure. "Do not rebuild the pipeline for this, use the existing export even though it is ugly."

Keep it to the two or three most likely, not every conceivable risk. A long risk list gets
ignored the same way a long doc does.

## Step 3: Show the gaps back before drafting

Before writing anything, tell the user which of the six they could not answer crisply. This is
the most useful thing in the skill and it takes three lines.

An unanswerable question is not a gap in the brief. It is a gap in the thinking, and it will
surface later as rework, a stalled task, or a frustrated person who did their best with what
they had.

Be specific about what it costs:

> Gaps I see:
> - **Why**: you said the exec team asked for it. If that is all they get, they will optimize
>   for looking thorough rather than for the decision. What is the decision actually riding on this?
> - **What great looks like**: no example and no format. This is the one most likely to come
>   back wrong. Is there a past version, even a bad one?
> - **Risks**: you mentioned Priya will have opinions. Worth telling them that upfront so they
>   loop her in early rather than getting blindsided in review.

Then either get the answers, or draft with the gaps flagged so the user can decide whether to
send anyway. Sometimes shipping an 80% brief today beats a perfect one on Friday. That is the
user's call, not yours. Just make sure they are making it knowingly.

## Step 4: Draft the handoff

Default to something they can paste into Slack or a doc. Match the weight of the brief to the
weight of the task: a two-hour task gets four lines, a quarter-long project gets the full
structure.

Lead with the ask, then the why. Do not bury the request under three paragraphs of context.

```
**What I need:** [the ask, in one sentence, with the deliverable named]

**Why it matters:** [the real reason, and what it unblocks or decides]

**What great looks like:** [example, link, or concrete description of the artifact]

**By when:** [date]. [Priority relative to their other work, and the quality bar.]

**What you will need:** [access, docs, people, decisions already made]

**Watch out for:** [the two or three most likely failure modes, and what not to do]

**Check in:** [when you want to see progress, and in what form]

**Before you start:** [ask for the two-sentence playback]
```

Drop sections that do not apply. A brief with an empty "watch out for" heading is worse than
one without the heading.

Keep the register conversational. This is a message to a colleague, not a work order. If the
user's other messages are casual, match that.

---

## Step 5: Set the autonomy level and the checkpoint

Two lines that prevent most of the remaining failure modes.

**Autonomy.** Say which one this is, in plain words:

- *Bring it back before you act.* Rare, and reserve it for genuinely irreversible things.
- *Decide, then tell me before it goes out.* The default for stretch work.
- *Decide and go, tell me after.* The default for wheelhouse work, and where you should be
  trying to get to over time.

Ambiguity here is what produces both flavors of pain: the person who ships something you would
have stopped, and the person who waits three days for permission you did not know they needed.

**The checkpoint.** Set one deliberate early check-in rather than hovering. The right moment is
when they have made their first real directional choice, and before they have built anything on
top of it. For a week-long task that is usually a day or two in.

Say what you want to see at that point: an outline, an approach, the first section. "Send me
your outline Tuesday" is a checkpoint. "Keep me posted" is not.

If it is a stretch assignment, add the invitation explicitly: "if you hit something that feels
ambiguous, that is expected, come ask rather than guessing."

**Say when you would step in.** Name the condition that would make you take the work back or
get involved: a date slipping, a specific stakeholder escalating, a decision that turns out to
be bigger than the task. Stated upfront it is a safety net. Discovered in the moment it feels
like you never trusted them.

### Ask for the reverse-brief

The single cheapest thing in this whole skill: before they start, ask them to play back what
they are going to do and why, in their own words. Two or three sentences, not a document.

> "Before you dive in, tell me back how you are thinking about this so I can check I briefed
> it properly."

Note the framing. You are testing your brief, not quizzing them, and saying so is what keeps it
from landing as a lack of confidence in them.

This is also the only thing that cleanly separates the two failure modes you will otherwise
argue about later. If the playback is right and the output still comes back wrong, that is an
execution or capability gap. If the playback is already wrong, your brief failed and you just
caught it for free, before they spent a week on it.

## When the user is already in the rework loop

Some users arrive after the failure: "I keep having to redo their work." Do not jump to
drafting a better brief. Diagnose first, by running the last failed handoff back through the
six questions.

Almost always one of these:

- **The why was missing.** They made reasonable local decisions that were wrong globally.
- **Great was never defined.** They produced a perfectly good thing that was not the thing.
- **The autonomy level was unstated.** They over- or under-reached, and both look like a
  judgment problem when they are a briefing problem.
- **It really was a stretch too far.** No brief fixes a capability gap. Say so plainly, and
  move to scoping down or pairing rather than re-briefing.

When the user genuinely cannot tell which of these it was, stop guessing and tell them to run
the reverse-brief on the next attempt. It settles the question in one exchange instead of
another wasted cycle, and it is a far better answer than a confident diagnosis you cannot
support from the outside.

Name which one it was. The point is not to relitigate the past task, it is that the next brief
should close that specific gap. And if the user is running the same pattern with several
people, that is a signal about their delegation habits, not about the people. Say that kindly
and directly.

## Delegating to an AI agent

Same six questions, same failure modes, one difference: an agent will not stop to ask. It will
confidently fill every gap you leave with a plausible guess.

So the two that matter most are what great looks like and what is most likely to go wrong.
Give an example of the output you want, and state your constraints as prohibitions, because
"do not touch the auth module" lands harder than a hope that it will not.

---

## What to avoid

**Do not write the brief for the user in their absence.** If they cannot answer why, do not
invent a plausible why. Ask. An invented rationale is worse than a missing one, because it
sounds right and sends the person in a confident wrong direction.

**Do not turn a four-line handoff into a one-page document.** The structure is a checklist for
you, not a mandatory template for them. Over-briefing a simple task signals distrust and is its
own form of failed delegation.

**Do not confuse delegation with abdication.** Handing over the task does not hand over the
outcome. If the user talks about the work as fully off their plate, and they are still
accountable for it, name that.
